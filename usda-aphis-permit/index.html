What is a USDA APHIS permit, and how is it different from FDA registration?

For a product with animal-origin ingredients, FDA registration alone is usually not enough. Two separate agencies review two separate things: FDA looks at food safety and the label, while USDA APHIS looks at the risk of the product carrying animal disease into the United States. When one is completed and the other is skipped, the shipment may be held at the port of entry.

Three abbreviations are often confused. Here is the difference:

  • USDA: U.S. Department of Agriculture.
  • APHIS: the animal and plant health inspection unit within USDA.
  • VS (Veterinary Services): the team within APHIS responsible for import permits for animal-origin products. This is the office that reviews the application, and the permit document is called the VS Import Permit.

The reason for the permit is biosecurity: the possibility that diseases such as foot-and-mouth disease, avian influenza and BSE could be carried through animal products keeps these imports subject to a permit. That is why the decision depends less on what the product is and more on the animal health status of the country of origin and how far the product has been processed.

Which products need it?

If a product contains an ingredient derived from an animal or in contact with an animal-origin source, it is likely to require a permit. The most commonly missed case is not treating the product as food: even a gelatin capsule in a supplement can trigger the process.

Pet food and dog chews

Dry and wet food, treats, pizzle, trachea, tendon, ear and hide-based natural chews. This is the group where a permit is most often required.

Animal by-products

Meal, fat and protein concentrates from meat, poultry and fish; bone, horn and hoof.

Gelatin, collagen and dairy derivatives

Gelatin, collagen peptides, milk powder, whey and supplements containing them.

Raw materials and textile inputs

Hides, skins, wool, feathers and hair. Even though they are not food, they may be subject to a permit because they are of animal origin.

How is the permit obtained in four steps?

01

Determine whether the product requires a permit

APHIS’s VS Permitting Assistant tool shows whether a permit is required based on the product type, country of origin and degree of processing. If this step is skipped, either an unnecessary application is filed or a required permit is missed. We handle the classification.
02

Open the account and build the application file

The application requires an eAuthentication account. The product description, animal species, processing method, packaging and annual volume are entered at this stage; the clarity of this description is what most affects the approval time.
03

Submit VS Form 16-3 through APHIS eFile

Since May 31, 2022, new and renewal applications have been filed through APHIS eFile ; the old ePermits system is no longer used. Import permits for animal products and by-products are requested with VS Form 16-3 . Older online content describing ePermits is no longer valid.
04

Receive the permit number and prepare the shipping documents

An approved permit receives a permit number, and that number accompanies every shipment. A veterinary health certificate from the country of origin and an Importer of Record in the U.S. are also required. If you do not yet have a U.S. importer, we can handle importer registration and the customs bond for your U.S. company or buyer.

What Does “USDA Certificate” Usually Mean?

What people search for as a USDA certificate is usually one of two different documents, and each comes from a different agency through a different process:

  • USDA APHIS permit (VS Import Permit, VS 16-3): the import permit required for products containing animal-origin ingredients such as meat, dairy, eggs, offal or fish oil, as well as pet food and treats, to enter the United States. The application is filed through the APHIS online system; the APHIS VS Permitting Assistant tool shows whether a product requires a permit. This is the service Export Partners provides.
  • USDA Organic (NOP) certification: the certification needed to sell a product as “organic” in the United States. USDA does not issue this certificate itself; independent certifying agents accredited by USDA issue it after an on-site inspection and renew it with an annual inspection.

For a product with animal-origin ingredients, a USDA APHIS permit alone is not enough; the facility also needs FDA registration and the product must meet U.S. labeling rules. For pet food and treats, the usual order is: FDA facility registration first, then a label compliant with U.S. rules, and finally the USDA permit application. Following this order keeps the product and facility details in the application file consistent from the start.

Frequently asked questions

Are the USDA permit and FDA registration the same thing?

No, they are two separate agencies and two separate applications. FDA registers the facility and reviews the label; USDA APHIS reviews the product's animal disease risk and issues the import permit. Products with animal-origin ingredients often need both.

How do I obtain a USDA VS Permit?

First, the APHIS VS Permitting Assistant tool is used to determine whether the product requires a permit; then an eAuthentication account is opened and the application is filed through APHIS eFile with VS Form 16-3. A veterinary health certificate from the country of origin and the details of a U.S. importer are added to the application.

Is the application still filed through ePermits?

No. According to APHIS’s own notice, new and renewal applications have been filed through APHIS eFile since May 31, 2022. Content describing ePermits is outdated.

I plan to export pet food. Is a permit required?

Pet food and chew products with animal-derived ingredients are likely to require a permit. The definitive answer depends on the product type, animal source and degree of processing; we classify the product through the VS Permitting Assistant and confirm the result in writing.

Should the manufacturer or the importer obtain the permit?

The permit runs through the party importing into the U.S., and the application requires an Importer of Record. The importer of record is your own U.S. company or your U.S. buyer; we handle importer registration and the customs bond on its behalf.

The permit is issued. Will the product enter the U.S.?

The permit is required but not sufficient on its own. On the customs side, importer registration and a customs bond are needed, and food products also require Prior Notice. If these steps are missing, a shipment may be held even with a permit in place.

How long does a USDA permit take?

Timing depends on the product class and APHIS’s workload at the time; there is no guaranteed figure. In practice, the most common causes of delay are an incomplete product description in the application and a late veterinary certificate.

Can I get the service as a package?

Yes. The USDA permit can be obtained on its own; FDA registration, label review, customs bond and shipping coordination can be added to the same package as needed.

Are USDA Organic certification and the USDA APHIS permit the same thing?

No. The USDA APHIS permit (VS 16-3) is an import permit that allows an animal-derived product to enter the U.S. USDA Organic (NOP) is a certification for selling a product as organic, issued after inspection by independent bodies accredited by USDA. A non-organic pet food may need an APHIS permit but not an organic certificate; an organic olive oil needs an organic certificate but not an APHIS permit.

In what order are FDA registration, labeling and the USDA permit handled?

For pet food and animal-derived food, the most reliable order is: first the FDA registration of the production facility and the US Agent appointment, then packaging prepared to U.S. labeling rules, and finally the USDA APHIS permit application. Since the permit application asks for facility and product details, settling registration and labeling first keeps the file consistent from the start and reduces the risk of follow-up correspondence.

Which rules apply to pet food labels in the U.S.?

In the U.S., pet food labels are prepared according to FDA rules and the AAFCO model regulations adopted by most states. The label includes the product name, net quantity, guaranteed analysis (protein, fat, fiber, moisture), ingredient list, feeding directions and manufacturer or distributor information. We can run the label compliance review in the same package as the USDA permit and FDA registration.
