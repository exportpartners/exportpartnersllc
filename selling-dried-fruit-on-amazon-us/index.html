Selling Dried Fruit on Amazon: A Real Export Story to the U.S.

Selling dried fruit on Amazon has been one of the topics we hear about most from manufacturers in recent years. The reason is clear: the freeze-dried snack shelf in the U.S. is growing quickly, but it is also crowded. Established brands like Crispy Green, and even giants like Hershey, have entered this category. So this is not a market you can enter by thinking "my product is good, I'll list it and it will sell"; alongside a good product, you need the right setup.

On the other hand, the picture is attractive. Dried food is a growing category in the U.S., especially on the snack side, and fruit is one of its standout products. No added sugar, single ingredient, vegan.. all three features American consumers look for are already built into freeze-dried fruit. For manufacturers with strong fruit quality and processing infrastructure, we think a well-built brand can realistically find a place on this shelf.

In this article we focus on practice rather than theory. We share, step by step, what we did while helping a manufacturer we work with bring its freeze-dried fruit line to Amazon U.S., where we stopped to think things through, and what findings came out of the case.

Why Amazon? Why the U.S.?

The traditional route for food exports to the U.S. is finding a distributor; but distributor negotiations take a long time, a significant share of the margin stays with the intermediary, and your brand often remains invisible. Selling food on Amazon U.S. gives manufacturers the chance to start under their own brand, at their own price, in direct contact with consumers. You can test with a small batch, see from sales data which flavors perform, and then approach distributors with much stronger data in hand.

Dried fruit has one more advantage on Amazon: it is not a gated category requiring approval, like pet food or dietary supplements. So if you have set up the FDA side correctly, the path to listing is relatively open. This is an important sequencing hint for manufacturers with more than one product line under the same roof; we will come back to it in the findings.

Case Study: A Manufacturer's Amazon U.S. Journey

One of the manufacturers we work with decided to bring its freeze-dried fruit line to Amazon U.S. under a U.S. LLC. The product range extended from single packs to multi-pack formats; the goal was to build a clean-label brand that fits into the American consumer's daily snacking routine.

The process moved forward under five headings. Let us explain what was done under each, and why it was done in that order.

1. U.S. Company and EIN: Unexciting but Essential Groundwork

The first step is always the same: forming a U.S. LLC and obtaining an EIN from the IRS. It is not the exciting part, we know. But the Amazon seller account, U.S. bank account, payment infrastructure and trademark application.. all of them sit on this foundation. In our case, because the LLC and EIN were completed early, none of the later steps stalled while "waiting for paperwork."

2. Trademark: Opening the Brand Registry Door Early

On Amazon, visibility on the snack shelf comes through Brand Registry: A+ content, a brand store, video, tools for protection against counterfeits.. all of these are unlocked by this program. Brand Registry, in turn, requires a trademark that is registered or has a pending application in the U.S.

In this case, the manufacturer filed a USPTO application in Class 29 (freeze-dried fruits) for its fruit brand before the product ever shipped to the U.S. This early move meant Brand Registry was ready on launch day and brand awareness could start building from day one. For details, see our U.S. Trademark Registration guide and our Amazon Brand Registry guide.

3. FDA: The Real Gateway for Food Exports to the U.S.

No matter how good the Amazon listing is, it means nothing if the product cannot clear the U.S. border. In food exports to the U.S., dried fruit is treated by the FDA as a conventional food, and the following obligations apply:

  • Food Facility Registration + US Agent: Your production facility must be registered with the FDA and must designate a U.S. Agent. Without registration, the product cannot enter the U.S.
  • Prior Notice: Electronic advance notice to the FDA and customs before every shipment.
  • FSVP: In U.S. food imports, the importer is expected to maintain a Foreign Supplier Verification Program (FSVP). This obligation belongs to the importer, not the manufacturer; if you import through your own LLC, it falls on you, and if a U.S. buyer imports the goods, it falls on them.
  • Ingredient compliance: For single-ingredient fruit, this part is usually simple; but if flavorings, coatings or blends are involved, every component must be acceptable to the FDA.

One fact made our work easier in this case: fruit is a plant-based product. The USDA/APHIS permits and health certificate layer that comes up with meat-based products never applied here. For the practical steps on the FDA side, we recommend our FDA Registration and Certification guide and our FDA Labeling and Customs Service Guide.

4. Labeling: The Retail Unit Question

Every manufacturer selling a multipack needs to answer one question up front: which package do FDA labeling rules apply to? The answer: the retail unit the consumer buys. If the box is what gets added to the cart on Amazon, all mandatory elements must be on the box: statement of identity, net quantity (grams + oz), ingredient list, allergen declaration, Nutrition Facts in the current format (including the added sugars line), manufacturer/distributor information and country of origin. The individual packs inside can stay simpler if they are only sold within the box; but if you ever list a single pack separately, that pack must be fully compliant on its own. The entire label is in English. Settling this question before box design begins removes the cost of revisions after printing.

5. Amazon Listing and Launch: Where the Real Game Begins

Once the regulatory foundation is set up correctly, the listing side moves quickly on a technical level; but listing is not the same as selling. Standing out on a crowded snack shelf takes the right launch plan, PPC ad management and organic visibility work. Practical points we watch for in Amazon FBA food shipments:

  • Lot number and expiration date must be clearly legible on the packaging; FBA warehouses do not accept food without an expiration date
  • Packaging should be original and sealed; a crush-resistant box design directly affects the return rate
  • Avoid unsupported health claims in listing copy; phrases like “boosts immunity” can cause problems with both the FDA and Amazon
  • If Brand Registry is active, raise conversion with A+ content and a comparison chart

For the full launch and listing strategy, our Amazon Private Label Starter Guide is a good starting point.

6. Amazon Search Terms: How Do Customers Find You?

Selling on Amazon starts in the search box. American shoppers don't search for a literal translation of “dried fruit”; they search in their own words and around their own use cases, such as freeze dried strawberries, healthy snacks for kids, no sugar added fruit snacks or smoothie topping. Thinking in your own language and translating word for word is one of the most common mistakes we see.

The practical approach we follow for search term research:

  • Competitor analysis: Use tools like Helium 10 or Jungle Scout to find which keywords bring traffic to the best-selling ASINs in the category
  • Discovery with an automatic campaign: Run an automatic PPC campaign at launch to see which searches Amazon matches the product to; move the converting terms into a manual campaign
  • Listing placement: Place the main keywords in the title and secondary keywords in the bullets and backend search terms field, without turning the title into a keyword pile
  • Negative keywords: Cut the click cost from irrelevant searches early

This cycle is not a one-time task; as search term data builds up over the first 8-12 weeks, the listing and ad structure are refined together.

7. Concepts That Stand Out on a Crowded Shelf

In freeze-dried fruit, the product itself is easy to copy; the difference comes from concept and positioning. Approaches we have seen gain traction in the U.S. market:

  • Positioning by use case: The same product can be sold to very different audiences as a “lunchbox snack”, “office snack” or “post-workout” snack; the listing and images are built around that moment
  • Variety pack: Letting customers try different fruits in a single pack makes the first purchase decision easier and raises basket value
  • Topping concept: Positioning the product as a complement to smoothies, yogurt and granola rather than as a snack opens the door to a different stream of search traffic
  • Kids and family focus: No added sugar and a single ingredient tend to resonate strongly with parents; the packaging language is simplified accordingly
  • Gift and seasonal: Themed packs for periods such as back-to-school and the holiday season can give an everyday product seasonal momentum

Concept selection is not arbitrary; the decision is made by weighing search volume data, competitor density and the manufacturer's actual production flexibility together.

The Roadmap at a Glance

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Do you need FDA approval to sell dried fruit on Amazon?

FDA does not approve products one by one; however, obligations such as facility registration, US Agent, prior notice and FSVP apply. Once these are in place, dried fruit enters the U.S. as a conventional food.

Is dried fruit a gated category on Amazon?

No. Unlike pet food or dietary supplements, dried fruit snacks for human consumption are generally not subject to a pre-approval process. This is one of the factors that can speed up the launch.

Does the multipack box also need a U.S.-format label?

Yes. If the unit the consumer buys is the box, all mandatory elements must appear on the box. The inner packs can be simpler if they are sold only inside the box.

Is a USDA permit required?

Because dried fruit is plant-based, the USDA/APHIS layer generally does not apply; those requirements are for animal products.

Can the label be in another language?

No. The entire label must be in English and include all mandatory elements.

5 Takeaways from This Case

As a team that has been through the process end to end, here are the takeaways we distilled from this case for manufacturers preparing for a similar journey:

  • 1. Sequencing is strategy. The LLC → trademark → FDA → label → Amazon order is not arbitrary; each step is a prerequisite for the next. Manufacturers who break the sequence often end up in a “product ready, but account/documents pending” loop.
  • 2. A plant-based product can be the fastest entry point. If several product lines sit under one roof, launching with a fruit line that needs no USDA layer and is not gated lets you activate the account early and collect sales data while preparing the more heavily regulated lines in parallel.
  • 3. The retail unit question on the label should be settled before printing. In multipack structures, if the question “which pack carries the mandatory elements?” is not answered at the design stage, post-print revision costs become hard to avoid.
  • 4. Search term research should start before launch. A listing that is not built around the American consumer's actual search language (such as freeze dried strawberries, healthy snacks for kids) can leave even the best product invisible. Filing the trademark early also helps ensure Brand Registry is active on launch day.
  • 5. Listing is not selling. Once the regulatory work is complete and the product is on the shelf, the real game begins: the right concept, a listing built on the right search terms, PPC ad management, organic visibility work, social media traffic, and demand driven from outside Amazon through an SEO-focused website. On a crowded shelf, the difference comes from the work done here, and we support our manufacturers in each of these areas.
